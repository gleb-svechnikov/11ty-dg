Cultivating Ideas: Exploring Digital Gardening as a Tool of Personal Information Management in Communities of Practice | MBIB4250-1 Essay

1 Introduction 

In this essay, I will focus on introducing two important subjects covered in the course Knowledge Management and Information Behavior – Communities of Practice (CoP), and Personal Information Management (PIM). I will also frame it within the broader context of Knowledge Management in general. I will not only describe those two fields more thoroughly, but I will aim to link them together and find possibilities of their intersections. 

To anchor these abstract concepts in a concrete context, I will examine these phenomena through the lens of a specific example – the community of community curators and digital gardens. These concepts will be explained in detail as we progress through the essay, serving as a practical case study for the theoretical exploration.

I would like to try to find an answer to my research question, which is: How can the concept of Digital Gardening enhance knowledge management and personal information management within Communities of Practice?

The justification for my research question is the growing importance and potential impact of effective knowledge management within the communities of practice. (Amin & Roberts, 2008) Groups of individuals from different disciplines come together to share knowledge and learn from each other. Therefore, the methods they use to manage and navigate their shared information and both their explicit and tacit knowledge can significantly impact the effectiveness and productivity of their community.

Digital gardening can be perceived as a very flexible, organic approach to personal information management. (Appleton, 2020) I use it as an important issue in this essay to show, what potential innovative solutions it could bring to communities, and how it could enhance their knowledge management. To counterbalance the benefits that this concept can bring to communities, I will certainly mention the pitfalls and problems that come with this kind of personal information management. 

Through my research in this essay, I aim to bridge the gap between communities of practice, PIM of the members of CoP and their collective knowledge sharing. However, the application and potential benefits of the concept of digital gardens in the context of CoP remain unexplored. Nevertheless, there are other valuable existing examples of knowledge sharing and management, that I will draw from and try to link to the digital gardening concept. 

Through this theoretical investigation, I hope to contribute valuable insight that could enrich current concepts and practices. The findings could offer practical strategies for communities to manage their knowledge sharing and foster knowledge interactions among members.

1.1 Background and Key Definitions

Before I delve deeper into the methods, research and discussion of the results, I would like to address some of the key definitions and terms that will be used throughout this essay. A clear understanding of these concepts is the first step towards ensuring a comprehensive and coherent exploration of the topic. This framing will provide a solid foundation to help interpret and understand the more complex arguments and discussions that will follow.

1.1.1 Communities of Practice

A Community of Practice (CoP) is a group of people who share a common interest, profession or passion and actively learn and develop from each other. (Barab & Duffy, 2012) Members of CoP often frequently interact with each other: they share knowledge, inspiration, and information. That could be perceived as a common engagement in the learning process of the whole community. (Wenger-Trayner et al., 2023)

This term was first mentioned by anthropologist Lave and computer scientist Wenger in the early 1990s in their book Situated learning: legitimate peripheral participation. (1991) In this book, they opened the topic of the community and social aspects of the learning process.

Nevertheless, Brown, Collins and Duguid (1989) have previously stated that knowing and doing something are two reciprocal activities.  According to them, knowledge is situated in place and time and gradually developed through action. This theory implies that engagement in practice can lead to understanding and learning.

Such a community of practice can exist in various forms: it could be, for example, a team of researchers working remotely on collaborative research, a team of staff from an organisation who meet every day in a meeting room, a community of local knitters who organize a meet-up once a week at the public library, or perhaps a group of people with the same interests united in a Facebook group. 

1.1.2 Personal Information Management

Personal information management (PIM) can be briefly defined as a certain set of activities that are performed to acquire, store, organise and maintain information. (W. Jones, 2007) Jones also adds: “One ideal of PIM is that we always have the right information in the right place, in the right form, and of sufficient completeness and quality to meet our current needs.” (2007)

The activities of PIM have as a main goal to manage and organize information in a way, that would meet the personal needs and preferences of an individual. Jones in his next book on personal information management  The Future of Personal Information Management later states: “PIM is the art of getting things done in our lives through information.” (2012) As well as that, PIM ensures that the desired information is accessible and easily retrievable when needed. Jones and Teevan describe in their book the issue of scattering diverse pieces of information and the chaos it ignites. They also propose personal information management as a tool to remedy this and improve organisation and efficiency. (2007)

This can both increase efficiency but also improve the feelings of the individual. Alon and Nachmias explore this issue in their article Anxious and frustrated but still competent: Affective aspects of interactions with personal information management. (2020) In this paper, the authors describe the development of a new theoretical framework for the affective aspects of PIM. They attempt to justify and explain all the methods of the study, which aimed to explore individuals’ experiences and emotions associated with PIM. One of the important findings is that the form of interface or platform used for PIM has an impact on emotions. The issue of platforms will be revisited later in the issue of digital gardens and the digital environments where they can be created. 

1.1.3 Digital Gardening Concept

Digital gardening is a concept within personal information management (PIM) that analogizes the handling of personal digital information and knowledge to maintaining a garden. It is an approach to organizing and understanding information that encourages the active cultivation of ideas, much like gardeners tend to cultivate their plants. There is also a strong emphasis on openness and sharing with the public. This approach works with thoughtful and engaged interaction with information. In digital gardens, ideas have space to evolve, connect and grow organically. (Appleton, 2020)

Contrasting with traditional approaches, which are often linear or hierarchical, the digital gardening approach offers greater flexibility. Yet even here we can observe some principles of hierarchy within this garden metaphor. Just as elements in a physical garden follow a progression of growth, knowledge and ideas in a digital garden environment similarly evolve and get organised. 

We begin with small seeds, seedlings, and sprouts – these often represent undeveloped ideas or notes. Perhaps something has piqued our interest, and we have decided it might be something we would like to cultivate in the future, even though we do not have a precise plan for it yet. So, we place it in a digital flower bed to see if it will grow. If it does, we might label this idea as a bindweed or a budding branch, indicating that we have expanded on the original idea and the structure is beginning to grow.

At the peak – or perhaps at the root – of this garden hierarchy is the tree. This represents a large, developed, and deeply rooted idea, or even a whole body of knowledge on a topic. It has likely been growing in the garden for several years, receiving constant care and attention from the gardener.

This metaphor has a deeper knowledge management grounding. Caufield (2015) brings to the table the concept of this topological organization of digital knowledge, sparking the discussion on the topic of digital streams. In his article on the subject, he even compares the function of digital gardens with Vannevar Bush’s Memex (1996). He suggests that if Bush’s visions were ever fully realized, they would represent the “ultimate garden”.  (Caulfield, 2015) I will discuss more PIM case studies based on the digital gardening concept later in the Results section of this essay. 

1.1.4 Community Curators

In this essay, I operate with the term community curator— a phrase that might seem abstract and elusive to many. Those eager to delve into its definition through conventional means such as literature or articles might find themselves at a dead-end and be quite disappointed. The term community curator is not widely recognised or used. In fact, its use can be traced back to a group of information scientists in Brno, Czechia who began using it a few years ago in their work with community librarians. (Víchová, 2018) They also created a course of the same name at Masaryk University, where students can learn and develop in the topic of community work. 

The role of community curator is often connected to the role of community organiser (Minkler, 2019), community manager (Markovets et al., 2020) or sometimes even community leader (Jimenez et al., 2019), but it still holds its unique function which is slightly different from all of those. Unlike those mentioned above, the role of a community curator is defined not only as a person who helps organise activities in the community, but who consciously connects its members, tries to stand within the structure of the community and sparks feelings of belonging, ownership, and engagement in its members. (Škyřík, 2024)

Despite its limited usage, the concept of community curation holds significant value. Some of the key principles, requirements, and skills associated with being a community curator are stress resilience, leadership and teamwork soft skills, sufficient flexibility, adaptability to new realities, and strong emotional intelligence, values and ethics. (Škyřík, 2024)

The reason I mention community curators at all is because later in the discussion section of this essay I will present, in addition to the results of the literature, a small-scale thought experiment that operates specifically with a group of community curators. I chose this community of practice for the reason that I work with these people in my everyday life and teach them about how to build and nurture functional and healthy communities. I am well aware of their needs and wishes. 

Therefore, I found it fitting to be able to try to outline in this essay an imaginative solution for this group, which is currently struggling with the problem of a non-existent platform for sharing information and experiences. However, I devote only a small part of the whole essay to this part, which is otherwise based mainly on the results of literature research and review.

1.1.5 Knowledge Management

Knowledge management can be briefly described as the process of creating, sharing, using, and managing knowledge and information. It includes the strategies and practices used to identify, analyse, capture, structure and distribute knowledge in order to improve organisational performance and create values. Despite its complexity, at its core, knowledge management seeks to utilize collective knowledge to achieve goals. (Rasmussen & Nielsen, 2011; Wallace, 2007)

This phenomenon is often associated with organisations and companies where it can play a very important role. (Erden et al., 2008) According to Lundvall and Nielsen (2007), the original intent of knowledge management was to transform tacit, hard-to-transfer knowledge into explicit knowledge – that is, knowledge that can be easily communicated, classified and disseminated and it may enhance innovation within an organisation.

It is also a very cross-disciplinary issue: the discourse of information science, philosophy, economics, communication, education, and psychology meet in the field of knowledge management. The historical development of this phenomenon, its minor nuances as well as its larger sub-concepts are described in the comprehensive book Knowledge Management: Historical and Cross-Disciplinary Themes by Danny P. Wallace. (2007)

This phenomenon could certainly be explored in much more detail. However, in this essay, I focus only on small sections of it: personal information management and knowledge management of communities of practice. Nevertheless, I found it important to define and outline this framing concept at least briefly.

3.1 Personal Information Management

At the beginning of this essay, a preliminary definition and exploration of personal information management (PIM) was given, outlining its basic aspects and components. In this section, a more detailed examination of selected sources dealing with this topic will be undertaken to offer a deeper understanding of PIM within academic discourse.

One of those sources can be a chapter of the Annual Review of Information Science and Technology Volume 41, Issue 1 called Personal Information Management. (W. Jones, 2007) The author describes that there are many different tools and technologies available for information management. These range from simple note-taking applications to complex project management software, each with its own strengths and weaknesses tailored to different use cases. However, it is essential to bear in mind that the landscape of these tools and technologies is evolving significantly over time. 

The publication referenced here is more than fifteen years old, a fact that requires consideration of the technological advances that have occurred since then. (W. Jones, 2007) The rapid pace of innovation has led to the development of more sophisticated and versatile information management tools that may not be considered in the context of the cited work. 

Nevertheless, the author makes an observation about the information fragmentation that could be encountered even then. This typically manifests itself when an individual uses multiple information management devices in the various roles they occupy. The use of different devices and platforms can lead to information scattering, making it difficult to maintain a coherent and comprehensive understanding of the information in question. (W. Jones, 2007)

As well as that, the author proposes a thought-provoking idea: “Information is a means to an end.” (W. Jones, 2007) However, with this quote, the author emphasizes the role of information as a tool for task performance and goal realization, applicable in both personal and organizational contexts. Personal information management (PIM) emerges as a major player in this scenario. PIM structures and organizes our information, thus promoting efficiency gains. It streamlines processes and simplifies information orientation, navigation and retrieval. By providing a systematic approach to information management, PIM enables us to manage data overload more effectively and use it more strategically to achieve our goals. Thus, the importance of PIM goes beyond the mere organization and affects our overall productivity and success in accomplishing tasks.

William Jones returns to the issue of PIM in his next book, The Future of Personal Information Management. (W. Jones, 2012) In this book, he describes in several chapters the possible future of PIM in what he calls “a second age of digital information.” (W. Jones, 2012) He describes the shift from desktop devices to much more subtle laptops and smartphones and how this is affecting individuals’ PIM. He delves deeper into the topic of wearable information devices in the chapter Our Information, Always at Hand.

In the initial chapter, the author provides an articulate definition of Personal Information Management (PIM), while also distinguishing between the concepts of knowledge and information and the related concepts of information and knowledge management. This introductory section not only offers a brief overview of the historical development of PIM but also addresses the implications of the information explosion and its pervasiveness in the everyday lives of individuals. The author highlights potential problems that individuals may encounter in their information management practices. These include the looming threat of information chaos, the struggle to locate relevant information in a timely and efficient manner, and the persistent nature of information stored on the World Wide Web, which may present its own unique set of sustainability issues. (W. Jones, 2012)

The issue of everlasting storing of data and information on the Web he further explores in the chapter Our Information, Forever on the Web. He perceives the Web as a place “where we might achieve a measure of immortality.” (W. Jones, 2012) The author uses this statement to highlight a fascinating and complex aspect of the second digital age – the persistent nature of the information we upload to the internet or the cloud. The implication is that the digital footprints we leave behind can potentially outlive us and remain on the Web for an indefinitely long time. Although we are not able to determine the exact lifespan of this digital data, the author hypothesizes that it could, in principle, exist almost indefinitely. This brings up interesting considerations about how we perceive and manage our digital information, given its potential to persist and be available long after we are gone.

All of these aspects that Jones describes in his books are interspersed with articles and studies by other authors. One of the earlier studies on PIM is The psychology of personal information management. (Lansdale, 1988) Although this study dates from an earlier time, it was ahead of its time in recognizing the need for new methods of organizing information and especially describing the psychological aspects of PIM. 

The study presents a progressive view of the transition from paper to paperless offices and addresses the psychological implications of such a transformation. This shift has implications not only for the way information is managed but also for how people work with and perceive information. Of particular note is the study’s focus on human perceptions of information classification. This involves understanding how individuals categorize, store and retrieve information, which is closely related to memory function. The study examines the human ability to recall details of things, processes and problems. This emphasis on the cognitive aspects of information handling highlights the inherent interplay between our mental processes and the way we handle information. (Lansdale, 1988)

As timeless as this study is, it is also important to draw on more recent sources, such as the paper Mood and personal information management: how we feel influences how we organize our information, published in 2020. (Whittaker & Massey, 2020) In it, the authors also look at human psychology and the experiences associated with PIM, but they mainly explore how mood as an emotional factor can influence our digital storing and management behaviour in PIM. The research has shown that positive moods generally lead to more inclusive categorising of information, whereas negative moods result in more fine-grained filing of information. 

This paper consists of two different studies. The first study explored the connection between neuroticism, which is often associated with negative mood and attitude, and the filing habits of individuals. It has been found that participants with more prevalent negative moods tend to create different organizational structures – mainly creating more folders with the content of fewer files and deeper folder structure. (Whittaker & Massey, 2020)

The other study was more of an experimental manner. It was based on the manipulation of individuals’ moods and observation of how it can affect and change and impact the organization of certain file collections. Aligning with the first study, it found that participants in negative moods tended to create more different folders containing fewer files. (Whittaker & Massey, 2020)

The important fact that the authors conclude from those studies is a critical challenge for the co-organization of collaborative files depending on the moods and emotions of individuals. (Whittaker & Massey, 2020) This insight ties in with the concept of communities of practice and their approach to collaborative information management, which I explore in more detail in the next chapter.

Already in the introduction of this thesis the study Anxious and frustrated but still competent: Affective aspects of interactions with personal information management (Alon & Nachmias, 2020) that also looks in more detail at the psychology and experience of individuals in relation to PIM. However, unlike the previous study, it does not examine the effect of mood on PIM, but the effect of PIM on the moods of individuals. 

The study proposes a theoretical framework for understanding emotional interactions with personal information management and identifies seven key emotional aspects: “anxiety, efficacy, frustration, desperation, belonging, dependence, and loss of control.” (Alon & Nachmias, 2020) The study found that the emotional experience of PIM varies based on gender, age and the number of platforms used for information management. Women were found to be more emotional as well as people using more platforms, but it decreased with age. 

All these insights and findings add a new layer to the complexity of our understanding of PIM. The studies suggest that platform design as well as future research on PIM should take into account the affective dimensions of individuals, as they can play an important role in the research issue.  

3.2 Communities of Practice

As I have already mentioned at the beginning of this essay, a community of practice can be defined as a collective of individuals who share a common interest, problem, or passion for a specific topic. Members of this community deepen their knowledge and expertise in this area through regular interaction. (Bolisani & Scarso, 2014)

In recent years, the role of technologies, when it comes to sharing knowledge within communities of practice, can neither be understated nor overlooked. A chapter from  Digital Habitats: stewarding technology for communities called Technology for Communities (Wenger et al., 2005) has already almost 20 years ago shown, that communities of practice rely heavily on a variety of technologies to foster a sense of unity and belonging that transcends temporal and geographical boundaries.

The authors emphasize the importance of technology in communities by presenting that technology can strongly and positively influence the sense of togetherness and belonging. They propose that technologies can help to bridge distances between people and help them build communities. At the same time they mention, that technologies may play an important role not only in the case of virtual communities but also in streamlining processes, information transfer and communication in face-to-face communities. From a present-day perspective, the fascinating thing about these statements is when they were made. In 2005, technology was much less prevalent than it is today – so the authors work with terms such as mailing list and when writing about diverse technological solutions, they mention that: “All this calls for new breeds of interfaces and devices that bring the experience of community to the individual.”  (Wenger et al., 2005)

Technology not only facilitates communication and interaction but also contributes significantly to the functioning and effectiveness of communities. This is also true in the context of knowledge management, where technology facilitates the creation, sharing and application of knowledge. Just as we discussed PIM in the previous section, here we can talk about what is called CIM, community information management. (Wenger et al., 2005)

However, the selection and integration of technologies present their own set of challenges. Finding a balance where technology supports the community without being the overwhelming focus is a difficult task that many organisations and communities both worldwide, but also in my close settings face. Wenger et al. mention, that “Communities cannot expect to have the full attention of their members nor can they assume that all their members have the same levels of commitment and therefore the same needs.” (2005)

As an example of both the issue of technological interaction, the need for new digital solutions and the fostering of a community through digital tools, we can consider the Scouting Institute of Brno (‘Skautský Institut v Brně’, 2024), a non-governmental organization with a focus on fostering a community that extends beyond scouts and guides to include a wider public with an interest in active citizenship and global awareness. Currently, they have a volunteer team of approximately 40 members who organize a variety of events and workshops for the general public.

Recognising the need for effective knowledge management within its team, the Scouting Institute decided to create a community information database on the platform of Google Drive. This database serves as a repository for all operational information, guides and manuals and resembles the structure of Wikipedia. However, as this is a purely internal matter, I cannot provide a link for viewing it. Nevertheless, I have personal experience using this database myself, as I volunteer at the Scout Institute.  

The introduction of this tool has had a major impact on the functioning of the community. It has facilitated the smooth integration and onboarding of new volunteers and team members by providing easy access to the information they need. The database streamlined internal processes and made communication more efficient, reducing the need to consistently engage other team members in information searches.

This example illustrates the potential of technology in improving knowledge management within communities and shows how it can assist in organizing, accessing and sharing information, thereby improving the overall efficiency and effectiveness of the community and the potential information behaviour of its members.

If we return from this minor practical side-track back to the communities of practice as such, we can find in the literature that these communities might also be called knowledge communities as Thomas Erickson and Wendy A. Kellogg mention in the chapter Knowledge Communities: Online Environments for Supporting Knowledge Management and its Social Context. (2002) They propose that collaboration and possible communication within a group is important. Moreover, they say: “(…) we are interested in designing on-line environments within which users can engage socially with one another, and, in the process, discover, develop, evolve, and explicate knowledge relevant to shared projects and goals.” (Erickson & Kellogg, 2002)

This phenomenon that Ericson and Kellogg describe can be seen in practice, for example, in the groups and communities that emerge on social media. As one example, I cite a study Facebook or fail-book: Exploring “community” in a virtual community of practice (Peeters & Pretorius, 2020) published by Cambridge University Press that looked at this issue.

Facebook or fail-book: Exploring “community” in a virtual community of practice 

This study examines how learners create a sense of community in virtual communities of practice (VCoPs) on Facebook and how this affects the interaction and learning process. (Peeters & Pretorius, 2020) Similar to CoPs, social interaction and group dynamics are expected to be ignited through individual community members in VCoPs. (Chrisentary & Barett, 2015) These communities also build on the knowledge they share through the virtual environment. 

In this study, two different groups of students learning English were used. They were two separate courses with two different teachers. Each of the teachers used Facebook as a social networking site in his class differently. “While both courses aimed to promote peer interaction and collaboration through the SNS, the student groups acted and interacted differently.”  (Peeters & Pretorius, 2020)

The results of this study showed that there was quite a big difference between the first and second group. In the first group, there were three subgroups: leaders, active members, and passive members. However, in the second group, only the teacher was at the core of the community and most learners only responded to his contributions. (Peeters & Pretorius, 2020)

The researchers conclude that this may have been mainly due to the fact that Facebook in this case served two purposes: community bonding and the transmission of educational materials. It is because of this that greater power inequalities (teacher versus learner relationships) may have emerged and become a barrier to greater student interaction. (Peeters & Pretorius, 2020)

In the study’s conclusion, the researchers also point out that design plays an important role in whether Facebook or another social networking site can work to build and strengthen a community of practice. They mention that it is advisable to use inclusive design, incentives, and motivation of individuals, or activities that focus on the personal contribution of learners on such platforms for better interaction. The role and profile of each participant and the purpose of the whole group are also important. “In the end, fostering peer interaction and learner participation online can only be successful if both teachers and learners find their place within the spaces that have been created.” (Peeters & Pretorius, 2020)

“The Art of Knowing”: Social and Tacit Dimensions of Knowledge and the Limits of the Community of Practice 

In the field of knowledge management, the theory of communities of practice has become an important theory. An exploration of this theory is presented in an article “The Art of Knowing”: Social and Tacit Dimensions of Knowledge and the Limits of the Community of Practice, in which Paul Duguid addresses the unique nature of knowledge within communities of practice. (2005)

Duguid discusses the issue of knowledge transfer between different communities. He highlights the problem of transferring so-called “best practices” between very different communities – a reductionist attempt to convert tacit knowledge into explicit knowledge by simple coding and vice versa. (Duguid, 2005)

Moreover, the author also examines differences between social capital theories and communities of practice, which he argues are more focused on more complex communication as well as organized knowledge sharing. He states, that a community of practice “(…) limits itself to communities and networks where practice is coordinated or at least shared.” (Duguid, 2005)

Duguid’s exploration of the community of practice highlights the complex nature of knowledge transfer and the limitations of reductionist approaches in transforming tacit knowledge into explicit knowledge. The study highlights the characteristic focus of communities of practice on overall complex communication as well as organised knowledge sharing. Among other things, it offers a nuanced understanding of knowledge management within communities of practice. (Duguid, 2005)

The Smart Forest Atlas Project

In the literature, we can also find an example of a CoP based directly on the Digital gardening phenomenon. The Smart Forest Atlas project, which was created as a participatory platform for researchers based on some of the principles of digital gardening. This platform assists in both searching and contributing to a collection of materials. In the paper Digital Gardening with a Forest Atlas: Designing a Pluralistic and Participatory Open-Data Platform (Westerlaken et al., 2022), the research team describes the design process of this platform, theoretically anchors the concept of digital gardening, and mentions some of the threats it involves. 

The authors of this paper come up with the hypothesis that while digital gardening can foster innovative ways of sharing and archiving open data, it can also potentially perpetuate colonial and anthropocentric practices. By this, the authors mean particular issues that are associated with real gardening in a Eurocentric historical context – such as colonialism plantations, proving power and inequality. The authors urge that these practices should not be transcribed through the metaphor of gardening into the care of digital gardens of knowledge. (Westerlaken et al., 2022)

The authors suggest that digital gardening should move towards “more pluralistic, participatory, and more-than-human engagements with open-data research platforms while contributing to epistemic justice.”(Westerlaken et al., 2022) The Smart Forest Atlas project comes with the idea of transforming research platforms that use open data. They are intended to become more inclusive and manageable thanks to the six design qualities identified.

The proposed six design qualities are “Building Relational Commons through Open Data”, “Enabling Epistemic Justice through Data Plurality”, “Expanding Participation through More-than-Human Data”, “Transforming Digital Infrastructures through Sustainable Data”, “Cultivating Wayfinding through Unexpected Data”, and “Moving to Praxis through Open Data”. (Westerlaken et al., 2022) I would like to briefly comment on those as they can be perceived as important design implications within the field of knowledge management systems and data sharing. 

They are supposed to help keep open commons, relations, and space for further development of the shared data, to enhance the sharing of local narratives and practices and thus contribute to building a democratic society. They aim with this platform to enable more-than-human actors to play their role in the contribution to the digital garden, and at the same time to lower and minimise digital waste, usage of energy and servers which could be caused by pluralistic download and storing of the same data sets. The system is designed in a way that encourages users to find new pathways, discover unexpected data and explore new connections and links. They left the chronological structure and used a tagging system instead. The last thing that the authors of this paper propose is the outreach of the platform into practice, connecting communities, stakeholders and other actors. The authors say: “Digital gardens, in other words, can become lived worlds.”(Westerlaken et al., 2022)

3.3 Digital Gardening

Imagine you are entering a garden, but it is not a typical garden, here each plant represents a piece of knowledge that is being cared for. Some are mature trees that represent strongly rooted and extended knowledge, while others are sprouting seedlings that represent new thoughts and ideas that you have just begun to explore. This garden is not static; it is a living, evolving and open space where ideas grow, intertwine, and sometimes even lead you to unexpected discoveries. Welcome to the digital garden.

As it was already mentioned, the concept of digital gardening is one of the concepts of personal information management. It is an approach that is based on treating information like plants in a garden – including the hierarchy, forms and caring. (Appleton, 2020) However, in this section, I would like to delve deeper into the topic and use the literature on the subject to describe some of the other details that are important for digital gardens.

You may have wondered what form information takes in digital gardens. Are they posts, essays, ones and zeros, or perhaps personal notes? Everything is possible. Appleton (2020) describes, that the notes in the digital gardens are very unlike blog posts on our personal web pages. Those tend to be lush, refined and polished. On the other hand, digital gardens often consist of published unfinished ideas that will evolve over some time. They are less rigid and less perfect than conventional personal websites. Notes in digital gardens do not emphasize completion and perfection, but rather a process of public learning. “Rather than presenting finished ideas, digital gardens grow content visibly over time.”  (Westerlaken et al., 2022) Critchlow describes, that keeping a digital garden is like talking to oneself rather than to a large crowd of people. He says that this is the biggest difference between digital gardening and blogging. (Basu, 2020; Critchlow, 2019)

Numerous strategies exist for building your personal digital garden, including choices regarding interfaces, digital environments, and the establishment of rules for its management. (Critchlow, 2019) You have the freedom to unveil its entirety or reserve selected parts of the garden for private reflection. Furthermore, you have the authority of customising the search and retrieval functions, especially for your own needs – but you can also think about visitors of your garden and their information needs. There are no rules, limits, or restrictions on the field of digital gardening, so you can plant and grow whatever you feel will make your garden flourish.

Another aspect that can be linked to maintaining a digital garden as a personal information management tool is the affection and emotions it can evoke. Alon and Nachmias (2020) explore the idea of different emotions being evoked by different information behaviour principles and the interaction of individuals with their personal information. They did an extensive study on the subject, which has already been mentioned. One of the most significant findings was the high level of anxiety individuals tend to feel at the thought of either losing their personal data or their digital platforms collapsing. However, the authors propose that anxiety connected to the long-term preservation of information and PIM “(…) may be inevitable and, as such, should be addressed in research and in platform design.” (Alon & Nachmias, 2020)

Another important discovery was an increased level of frustration. This is mainly caused by the need tomaintain and sort the large amount of data and information that people store. Cushing (2011) in his research Self extension and the desire to preserve digital possessions even adds that in both sociology and psychology, we sometimes operate with the term extended self. The author applies this concept to digital objects which can be used as self-extension for people and change or influence their information behaviour – especially when it comes to the idea of saving, storing, and retrieving information.

The last concept, that can be linked to building and keeping up a digital garden, is serendipity. A certain degree of serendipity can play an important role in the maintenance of one’s own digital garden. Even though serendipity as such can be hard to define, the surprise, which we often connect to it “(…) has the power to jog our minds, to prod us to think, and learn more.” (McCay‐Peet & Toms, 2015) They describe serendipity as experience and in their study, they further explore what aspects of both behaviour and environment may enhance our perception of serendipity.

Linking this study to the idea of digital gardening as a tool for personal information management, I would like to mention, how the authors of the study perceive serendipity on a micro-level. They say: “At the micro-level, serendipity also involves an interaction between individuals and their environment and our definition of serendipity, an unexpected experience prompted by an individual’s valuable interaction with ideas, information, objects, or phenomena, is designed to reflect this positive interaction.” (McCay‐Peet & Toms, 2015)

This describes very well the situations that can arise when building and then browsing your own digital garden. This is because we come into contact with different information, data and new discoveries that we have come across in the past. This is where the design of our digital garden plays an important role – it depends on whether the environment is set up appropriately and stimulates the desire to delve into it and explore and learn new information, or not. If yes, it can be a very good source for said moments of serendipity.

The digital garden serves a dual purpose. Not only does it meet our individual needs, but it also functions as a valuable information source for others. The ethos of disseminating knowledge and making the digital garden accessible to the public forms one of its foundational principles (Appleton, 2020). Further emphasizing this idea, Westerlaken et al. (2022) state: “This orientation towards data sharing further inspires the design of web-spaces for participatory reflection and collective knowledge generation that can further expand open-data principles for participatory research projects.” Thus, the digital garden embodies the spirit of collaborative learning and open data, promoting shared reflection and knowledge creation. 

4 Discussion 

Connection of PIM, CoP, and Digital Gardening

The exploration of PIM, CoP and digital gardening in this essay revealed interesting insights into the way we manage and share information. One of the significant findings relates to the role of emotions in knowledge management and information behaviour within PIM. This finding highlights the complexity of PIM and suggests that it is not just a theoretical rational concept, but that various emotional factors need to be accounted for within it. (Alon & Nachmias, 2020; Whittaker & Massey, 2020)

Studies have shown that not only are our previous emotions and moods important when engaging in PIM (Whittaker & Massey, 2020), but that the process of PIM itself can also evoke a diverse range of emotions. These can span from feelings of efficacy and belonging to feelings of frustration and anxiety. (Alon & Nachmias, 2020) Interestingly, participants of this study have also reported experiencing unpleasant emotions at the idea of potentially losing some of their personal information. 

Another important insight gained from this literature review is the versatility of communities of practice that can emerge and develop in a wide range of different digital environments. The research highlights the importance of technology and design choices in shaping the functional dynamics of these communities. (Erickson & Kellogg, 2002; Peeters & Pretorius, 2020; Westerlaken et al., 2022) The influence of these elements extends to how the platform facilitates the interaction and engagement of community members, which ultimately determines the effectiveness of knowledge exchange and collaboration.

In the digital age, online platforms have become powerful tools for fostering community building and managing collaborative knowledge. They offer some key advantages such as flexibility, the ability to connect individuals across geographic boundaries and thus also easy access. These digital spaces can be designed to encourage active participation, facilitate the sharing of ideas and foster a sense of community among their members. (Westerlaken et al., 2022; Whittaker & Massey, 2020)

Successful implementation of digital platforms in communities of practice depends on a variety of factors. These include the design and usability of the platform, the nature of the community, and specific practices related to knowledge sharing and collaboration. (Peeters & Pretorius, 2020; Westerlaken et al., 2022)

However, communities of practice also exhibit great potential in community building and collaborative knowledge management through digital platforms. (Chrisentary & Barett, 2015; Peeters & Pretorius, 2020; Wenger et al., 2005; Westerlaken et al., 2022)

Nonetheless, the essential research question of this essay was: How can the concept of Digital Gardening enhance knowledge management and personal information within Communities of Practice? After thoroughly reviewing numerous sources, I have arrived at the conclusion that the principles of digital gardening can indeed be effectively integrated into knowledge management and PIM strategies within a wide range of CoPs. Notably, digital gardening principles encourage active collaboration, note-taking, flexible organization of files and exchange of information, knowledge and ideas. (Appleton, 2020; Critchlow, 2019)

They promote a more flexible and less rigid approach to information management, which can influence the way individuals in these communities collect, store and retrieve personal information. (Westerlaken et al., 2022) These flexible principles can also be perceived negatively: they can cause chaos and information fragmentation, or unsustainable information management.  (Appleton, 2020; W. Jones, 2007, 2012; W. P. Jones & Teevan, 2007)

Digital gardens have also the potential to work as platforms for collaborative learning and sharing open data. These online spaces are not merely repositories of information but are conceived as dynamic, interactive spaces that can foster shared reflection and individual knowledge creation. With their inherent emphasis on accessibility and exchange, digital gardens promote transparency and inclusivity and allow users to delve into different topics, engage with different perspectives and contribute their unique insights. It is this participatory nature of digital gardens that makes them an effective tool for collaborative learning. (Appleton, 2020; Westerlaken et al., 2022)

Based on all these findings, I would like to conclude this section with a small thought experiment based on them.

The Digital Gardening Colony Thought Experiment

Imagine a group of community curators, as diverse as the communities they represent but unified in their shared role: they are the curators of their communities. Their responsibilities encompass igniting change and action, uplifting members, and, most crucially, strengthening the sense of belonging. Moreover, they create a so-called community of practice.

This collective, having recently completed a course on community curation, has gained deeper insights into their roles and the potential growth of their communities. The learning journey, however, should not end with the course. Moreover, the course should, among other things, stimulate a personal desire to explore the topic more deeply while sharing community tips with other course participants. 

Now picture a digital platform, a colony of individual gardens of knowledge, information, and insights. Each member maintains their digital garden, a personalized space for organizing and cultivating their unique understanding of community-related topics. These gardens, interconnected, form a larger community, encouraging knowledge sharing, collaboration, and collective learning. 

The individual act of notetaking and curating one’s digital garden can inspire the wider community. By inviting others into their gardens, community curators can share knowledge, learn from each other and improve knowledge management practices. Each garden, unique in its colours, textures and perspectives, invites exploration and personal enrichment. This shared landscape reflects the essence of public learning and encourages the mutual exchange of knowledge and inspiration for community curators on the techniques, community practices and interventions as well as related literature. 

Another important purpose is sharing and possible interaction between individual “gardeners”. A larger gardening colony could facilitate collaboration and interaction among its members, fostering a dynamic and vibrant community of learners and knowledge creators. At the same time, taking into account the specific community of community curators that creates this gardening colony, there could be a much deeper connection and understanding of the given topic, because the individual members of the community already know what knowledge on a general level is used in this colony. They might even find new ways of using this colony within their membership base in their own communities.

This brings us to another idea and an important aspect of colonies – something that in botanical terms might be called a “cross-pollination” of ideas. Because many ideas meet at once in one colony, they can easily cross, interweave, develop and connect. This could lead to new perspectives, original innovations, answers to questions and solutions to problems that may not have been apparent at first in the gardens as individual pieces of the colony. This process greatly expands learning opportunities, offering a wealth of resources and perspectives.

The digital gardening colony could also strengthen community ties and resilience. Shared maintenance of a larger garden colony can foster a sense of community, belonging, mutual responsibility and collective ownership. Thanks to this, the resilience of the entire colony is also strengthened – it is much more stable and durable than individual gardens. It can withstand changes, various challenges or loss of individual members while still maintaining the collective knowledge base. Community curators can therefore experience some of the community principles first-hand. (Škyřík, 2024; Víchová, 2018)

Over time, a gardening colony can grow and evolve, reflecting the community’s ongoing learning and development. This constant development can keep the community of practice alive, relevant, and engaging. A study by Erden et al. (2008) shows that digital technologies and tools can also help to some extent in the transfer and sharing of tacit knowledge and thus development of a given community.

However, there could also be various problems. I would like to mention, for example, the issue of information fragmentation (W. Jones, 2007) or the issue of information and data sustainability (W. Jones, 2012; Westerlaken et al., 2022), which I mentioned earlier. These problems could eventually lead to confusion, anxiety, chaos and entropy within the colony as a whole, but also among individual gardeners.

Although this is an imaginative thought experiment, I draw on the lived experience of working as and with community curators. At the same time, I draw upon a background in information science, personal information management concepts and speculative design techniques, that open new horizons and allow us to dream, imagine and extend ideas. 

“This form of design thrives on imagination and aims to open up new perspectives on what are sometimes called wicked problems, to create spaces for discussion and debate about alternative ways of being, and to inspire and encourage people’s imaginations to flow freely.” This proposes Dunne and Raby (2013) in their book Speculative Everything: Design, Fiction, and Social Dreaming. 

5 Conclusion 

This essay aimed to explore how can the concept of digital gardening enhance knowledge management and personal information management within communities of practice. Throughout the literature review, it has been shown that PIM, CoPs and digital gardening have several points of convergence. 

Studies indicate that PIM can be strongly influenced by individuals’ moods and can also affect their emotional experience. (Alon & Nachmias, 2020; Whittaker & Massey, 2020) However, PIM can also ignite the feeling of belonging (Alon & Nachmias, 2020) which can be linked to communities of practice, that aim to connect people with similar passions and interests and enable them to share knowledge and learn from each other.  (Barab & Duffy, 2012; Wenger-Trayner et al., 2023)

That can be linked to the practices of digital gardening, which ignites and enhances active collaboration and sharing of thoughts using digital platforms. (Appleton, 2020; Critchlow, 2019) Through Michelle Westerlaken’s study (2022), it was confirmed that the principles of digital gardening are also applicable to science and research.

Nevertheless, several problems arise with the use of digital gardening principles. These include information fragmentation and chaos that can arise due to the very flexible structure of the gardens. There is also the issue of data doubling and sustainability. (Appleton, 2020; W. Jones, 2007, 2012)

This literature review opens a wide range of possible issues for future research. For instance, it could be studied, how to implement digital gardening practices within a specific community of practice and how it influences its processes, knowledge management and sharing. Additionally, research could examine new digital tools and technological possibilities for digital gardening as a community of practice tool. One such use case for future research could be a study comparing digital gardening as a tool for building CoP and Facebook as a tool that works specifically with the potential for community building and knowledge sharing. Lastly, it could be also researched, how artificial intelligence, algorithmizing and automation could help keep up digital gardens, tame the chaos and help make PIM more efficient.

In the digital age of information exploration we are experiencing, the way we manage and share information and knowledge is becoming crucial. Digital gardening is one possible alternative approach that promotes a culture of openness, sharing and collaborative learning.  This can not only enrich our individual knowledge but also strengthen our collective knowledge. As we continue to explore and exploit the potential of digital gardening, PIM, inclusive design and CoP best practices, and the intersections of all these phenomena, we may discover new and innovative ways to work with information and knowledge.
